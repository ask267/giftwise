Giftwise: be the friend who remembers.

Giftwise turns the messy gift notes and birthday lists people already keep into a reminder dashboard and a team of AI agents that finds a gift that fits the person, the budget, and the number of days left, with a real place to order it.

AuthorArchita Kadam GitHub repopaste the repo link here (same one submitted on Canvas) AppRuns locally from a fresh clone. See the README (backend on :8020, frontend on :5190). Not hosted online. Modelgpt-6-luna for every agent, through Portkey (course key)

2 · Motivation

This project started with two real situations.

Far from family

My roommate and I kept having the same conversation: what to get our family members for their birthdays now that we live so far away from them. The hard parts were remembering in time, remembering what they had mentioned months ago, and finding something that could actually arrive by the date from hundreds of miles away.

The Silver cohort birthday cakes

In my SOM cohort (Silver), we get a birthday cake for everyone on their birthday. Someone, usually the cohort rep, has to keep a spreadsheet of birthdays, notice whose is coming up, and organize a cake in time, all year. The whole tradition depends on one person's spreadsheet.

How people handle this today

  • Gift ideas live in scattered places: the iPhone Notes app, screenshots, texts, a spreadsheet. When a birthday arrives, nobody can find "the thing she said she wanted in March."
  • Reminders come too late. A calendar alert on the day itself is useless if the gift needs a week to ship.
  • "Ask ChatGPT for gift ideas" gives generic lists. You retype the person every time, it doesn't know what you already gave them, it doesn't know your timeline, and it gives you no place to actually order.
  • Organizers carry it alone. A cohort rep, team lead or RA tracking dozens of birthdays has only a spreadsheet.

What remembering late costs, measured in the app's own catalog: with a $60 budget, remembering 14 days ahead leaves all 75 gifts possible. At 5 days, 61 remain. At 1 day, only 31 of 75 can still arrive, and every personalized, custom or handmade option is gone. Remembering early is what keeps the thoughtful options open, and that's the part Giftwise automates.

3 · What is new here

I compared Giftwise against what people use today:

Existing optionWhat it does wellWhat it doesn't do
ChatGPT / Gemini (asked for gift ideas)Fast brainstormingNo memory of the person across years, no knowledge of past gifts unless retyped, ignores whether something can arrive in time, no real store or local shop to order from
Amazon Wish List, Giftster, ElfsterShared wishlists, Secret Santa drawsThe recipient has to build the list; nothing for the giver's own private notes; no help choosing when there's no list; no timing
Calendar and birthday-reminder appsTell you the dateRemind you on the day, not on the last day to order; no gift help

DoorDash, Instacart, local floristsSame-day deliveryYou still have to know what to send and which shop near them is good

What Giftwise does that these don't, together in one place:

  1. Starts from the notes people already keep. Paste the messy Notes-app list, or upload a screenshot or a calendar or contacts export. An agent sorts it into people, ideas, things to avoid, past gifts and dates.
  2. Remembers across occasions. Dislikes ("NOT another hoodie"), sizes and gift history are saved, and the house rules block repeats and dislikes in code.
  3. Plans around the deadline. The number of days left decides which delivery windows are searched at all, and reminders fire on the last day to order, not the birthday.
  4. Finds real local shops near the recipient (bakeries, florists, cafés) for the same-day case: the long-distance situation from Section 2.
  5. Group use: one Christmas budget split across a list, "chip in with friends," and calendar reminders the organizer can share.

Each piece exists somewhere on its own. The new part is combining memory, deadline-aware planning and multi-agent shopping, with prompt-injection defenses, for the person doing the remembering.

Plain code where AI isn't needed: the orchestrator (days left → which shoppers run), the house rules (budget, dislikes including near-synonyms like hoodie/sweatshirt, repeat gifts), the calendar and contacts parser, the catalog keyword search, the cost cap and the reminders file. These are cheaper, faster and can't be talked out of the rules.

How they hand off: the orchestrator calls the Idea agent; its concepts go to every possible shopper at the same time (Python asyncio); their candidates pass through the house rules; survivors go to the Critic; picks are grouped by delivery window. Each step streams its status to the page as it happens (Figure 3).

Model choice: every agent uses gpt-6-luna through Portkey. The course key only allows that model, and it's the cheapest option ($0.10 per 1M input tokens, $0.50 per 1M output). I first built on gpt-5.6-luna and switched when I read the rules. The work is short structured tasks (sorting, brainstorming, ranking), not long reasoning, so a small model fits, and splitting the job across agents keeps each prompt small and focused.

One real run, step by step

Mom's birthday, 12 days away, $60 budget, vibes "sentimental" and "practical," location Glen Allen, VA. This is the actual run, not a mock-up.

1
Orchestrator: 12 days left, so all three delivery windows are possible. Glen Allen, VA is geocoded through OpenStreetMap. The spend cap is checked. There's no cached answer, so the agents run.
2
Idea agent (1 call): 6 concepts, starting from her saved wishlist item (pottery class): "A pottery class together," "A small family photo album with handwritten captions," "A birthday letter bundle from the family," "A planned birthday outing…"
3
Four shoppers in parallel. Same day found 1 candidate; 2–3 days found 3 and hid 1 suspicious listing (the planted "Luxe crystal gift box," Section 6); 7–14 days found 1; Nearby looked at 4 real Glen Allen shops and proposed 2, including Dalia's Ice Cream & Bakery, 5.6 km away.
4
House rules (code) checked all 7 candidates against her $60 budget, dislikes and gift history. None needed removing in this run. In the comparison in Section 7, these checks are why Giftwise had 0 violations.
5
Critic kept 4 and cut 3 with reasons. It cut the "Open-when letters kit" because the 7–14 day delivery window could arrive after her birthday, which is 12 days away, and the restaurant gift card as "less personal than the family keepsakes."

Result: 4 picks (2 local same-day, 2 shipped in 2–3 days), each with a store link, using 12 calls, 13,851 input tokens (2,120 cached), 3,164 output tokens: $0.0028 (Figure 7).

AI cost is not the constraint. At 10,000 users, the real costs would be hosting and the map service: OpenStreetMap's free geocoder allows about 1 request per second, so a large deployment would need a paid or self-hosted geocoder.

What I did to keep cost down

  • Cheapest allowed model for every agent, with short, focused prompts per agent.
  • Skip what can't help: shoppers for delivery windows that can't arrive in time never run. Measured: 1 window $0.00113 (4.5 calls), 2 windows $0.00174 (6 calls), 3 windows $0.00262 (11 calls).
  • Plain code instead of AI for calendar parsing, house rules, catalog search, reminders and orchestration.
  • Result cache: the same search for the same person on the same day is free (shown as "From cache · no AI calls"). Gateway caching also helped: re-running the comparison in Section 7 took 4 seconds because Portkey served identical requests from cache.
  • Prompt cache: instructions are static and come first. Most single calls are under the provider's ~1,024-token caching minimum, so cached tokens are usually 0; the Nearby agent's longer 4-call loop did hit the cache (2,120 cached tokens in the traced run).
  • Limits: each shopper may make at most 8 model calls and 12 tool calls; card notes only run when asked.

Did cost cutting hurt quality?

In one place, yes. The catalog search is a cheap keyword match, not an AI or embedding search, so it sometimes misses a good item and a delivery window comes back empty. In the traced run, the 7–14 day window had "nothing strong enough" (Figure 6), and in another run a gift card made the final list. Skipping windows and caching did not hurt: skipped windows couldn't arrive in time anyway, and cached answers are identical. Giftwise also costs about 6.6× more per search than one plain model call ($0.0100 vs $0.0015 for the 6 cases in Section 7). That's the price of the memory, timing and safety checks, and both are fractions of a cent.

Quality vs. how it's done today ("just ask ChatGPT")

I gave the same 6 test people (likes, dislikes, past gifts, wishlist, days left, budget) to plain gpt-6-luna and to Giftwise, then checked every suggestion with the same code (eval/compare.py; full output in eval/results.md).

The plain model suggested a "personalized leather golf scorecard holder" for Dad with 6 days left, and a handmade mug for Mom with 2. Read honestly: when everything is typed into the prompt, a modern model mostly respects dislikes and budget. My first version of the checker even flagged a false positive ("socks… safer than another hoodie"), which I fixed. Giftwise's real advantages are that the user doesn't retype anything (it remembers), it never proposes something that can't arrive, and every pick is orderable. This is one run of 6 cases, not a statistical study.

Time

The app measures its own task times (data/metrics.jsonl, excluding instant cache replays). From my own first session:

  • Messy notes → 5 people saved with their ideas: 19.6 seconds.
  • "Find gifts" → choosing a delivery option for a pick: 30.3 seconds.
Real user test (Tier 3 evidence). Have your roommate (and if possible the Silver cohort rep) try it for ~10 minutes. Before they start, ask: "How long did picking your last gift for family take?" Record their answer, their Giftwise times (footer, or http://localhost:8020/api/metrics), one quote, and a screenshot. Replace this box with: who they are, before vs. after time, and the quote.

Reach, for the cohort example

For the Silver cohort tradition, the workflow already works: add classmates (by hand, or from a calendar or contacts export), download the reminders file so the rep's calendar alerts them on the last day to order, and use the Nearby agent to find a bakery near campus. At about $0.002 per search, a year of cohort birthdays costs about 12 cents in AI, and the saved list can be handed to the next rep. Spreadsheet import is the missing piece (Section 8).

8 · Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • The main catalog is a demo: about 100 hand-written products with approximate prices. Store links open each store's own search for the item, not a live product page, and the app can't check stock or exact price.
  • Local shops can't confirm same-day delivery. OpenStreetMap knows a bakery exists, not whether it delivers today, so the Critic always adds "call first." Some areas have sparse map data.
  • No spreadsheet import yet. The cohort's birthday list is an Excel sheet, but the app imports calendar (.ics) and contacts (.vcf) files, not .xlsx or .csv.
  • Single user, local only: no accounts or sharing, the spend cap is global rather than per user, and the app is not hosted.
  • Keyword catalog search sometimes leaves a delivery window empty (Section 5).
  • Small evaluation: 6 test people and the author's own timings. A real user study is still needed.

With another month

  1. Live products: a shopping-search API or retailer feeds instead of the demo catalog, with real prices and stock.
  2. Spreadsheet import and shared lists for organizers: import the cohort's Excel file, assign a "cake captain" per week, and track who's covered.
  3. Delivery confirmation: link local picks to DoorDash or florist APIs to confirm same-day availability before suggesting them.
  4. Hosted, multi-user version with sign-in, per-user spend caps, and email or text reminders instead of a calendar file.
  5. A proper study: 10–20 people, time-to-gift and satisfaction before and after, plus a larger version of the ChatGPT comparison.
